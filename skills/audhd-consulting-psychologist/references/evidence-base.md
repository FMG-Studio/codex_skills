# Evidence base for AuDHD conversational support

Last reviewed: 2026-08-08.

This file is a routing map, not a static clinical textbook. Re-check the current version, status, population, and applicability of a source before making time-sensitive or high-stakes claims.

## Evidence limits

Direct intervention research for adults with co-occurring autism and ADHD is limited. Do not merge separate autism and ADHD findings and call the result AuDHD evidence. Many autism intervention studies are pilots, use measures not validated with autistic adults, or target outcomes that autistic people may not value.

## Core sources

### Co-occurring autism and ADHD

- Young et al. (2020), *Guidance for identification and treatment of individuals with attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder based upon expert consensus*. This is expert consensus, not a systematic intervention guideline. Use it for integrated assessment and care principles while labeling the evidence level. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7247165/>
- Children and Adolescents with Co-Occurring ADHD and Autism Spectrum Disorder (2025), systematic review of multimodal interventions. This is pediatric evidence and must not be generalized to adults without qualification. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12156790/>

### Psychological support for autistic adults

- Deakin et al. (2026), *Fit-for-purpose Psychological Interventions to Support the Well-Being of Autistic Adults: A Systematic Review*. Of 69 included papers, most were pilot or exploratory designs; only a minority used measures designed for or standardized with autistic people. Frequently documented adaptations included concrete language, visual supports, clear concise content, and autism-experienced therapists. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13180214/>
- NICE CG142, *Autism spectrum disorder in adults: diagnosis and management*. Last reviewed in 2025; recommends adapting care to communication and other needs and using condition-specific guidance for coexisting mental disorders. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg142/chapter/Recommendations>
- Camm-Crosbie et al. evidence synthesis on adapting mental-health interventions for autistic adults, including barriers created by non-adapted treatment and clinician knowledge gaps. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11286644/>

### ADHD treatment in adults

- Ostinelli et al. (2025), *Comparative efficacy and acceptability of pharmacological, psychological, and neurostimulatory interventions for ADHD in adults*, systematic review and component network meta-analysis of 113 randomized trials. It supports short-term symptom effects for stimulants; longer-term and quality-of-life evidence is more limited. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39701638/>
- BMJ (2025), *Benefits and harms of ADHD interventions: umbrella review and platform for shared decision making*. In adults, atomoxetine, CBT, methylphenidate, and high-quality amphetamine trials had at least moderate-certainty evidence for ADHD symptom efficacy. Do not turn population evidence into individualized prescribing. <https://www.bmj.com/content/391/bmj-2025-085875>
- NICE NG87, *Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: diagnosis and management*. Use for clinical pathways and monitoring; check its current update status before relying on it. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations>

### Autistic burnout, masking, and capacity

- Ali et al. (2025), *Burnout as experienced by autistic people: A systematic review*, synthesizing 48 studies and approximately 4,000 autistic participants. Reported contributors included sensory and social overwhelm, camouflaging, stigma, daily-life demands, and alexithymia; reported recovery supports included rest, solitude, sensory relief, self-understanding, and individual/community support. The samples were not representative of all autistic people, and burnout is not established as a formal diagnosis. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41207162/>
- Timmerman et al. (2025), systematic review of quality-of-life measurement in randomized mental-health trials for autistic adults. Only 5 of 19 included trials measured quality of life, reinforcing that symptom change and meaningful wellbeing are not interchangeable. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39434651/>

### Emotion regulation, alexithymia, and interoception

- Van Bael et al. (2024), systematic review and meta-analysis of subjective interoception and alexithymia. Associations depended on measurement; alexithymia related to interoceptive confusion and lower adaptive interoception. Do not equate alexithymia with autism. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39509403/>
- McDonald et al. (2024), meta-analysis of emotion dysregulation in autism. Use to support attention to regulation difficulties, not to assume that every autistic person is dysregulated. <https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613241257605>
- Kircanski, Lieberman, and Craske (2012), affect labeling during spider-phobia exposure. Labeling reduced later skin-conductance response but did not significantly reduce self-reported fear relative to other conditions. This narrow result does not validate emotion wheels generally. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4721564/>
- Hegefeld et al. (2023), experiments finding that emotion naming could impede subsequent cognitive reappraisal and mindful acceptance. Use as a reminder that labeling is context-dependent. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36043172/>

### Plutchik and emotion models

- Plutchik's wheel is a psychoevolutionary theory and vocabulary visualization, not an empirically settled map of eight biological emotions. Modern affective science still debates basic-emotion, dimensional, appraisal, and constructionist models.
- Six Seconds (2025) is a practical secondary explainer with commercial calls to action, not a clinical guideline or validation study. Its claims about exact opposites, dyads, and unchecked temporal intensification should be treated as model assumptions. <https://www.6seconds.org/2025/02/06/plutchik-wheel-emotions/>
- Adolphs and Anderson-related Annual Review synthesis (2025), overview of contemporary emotion science and continuing theoretical disagreement. <https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-psych-020124-023338>

### Self-harm and suicide safety

- Blanchard et al. (2021), *Risk of Self-harm in Children and Adults With Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis*. Use to justify taking direct safety assessment seriously; do not calculate an individual's risk from group prevalence. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8527356/>
- NICE NG225, *Self-harm: assessment, management and preventing recurrence*. It recommends assessing immediate physical and psychological safety, collaboratively addressing accessible means, involving support where appropriate, and using a jointly developed safety plan. It explicitly advises against using risk scales or global low/medium/high categories to predict suicide or decide access to care. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225/chapter/recommendations>
- WHO, *mhGAP Intervention Guide, version 2.0*, includes emergency presentations and referral pathways for medically serious self-harm, imminent suicide risk, acute psychosis, intoxication, overdose, withdrawal, agitation, and violence. The guide is written for trained non-specialist health workers; an AI should use it only to recognize urgency and route to human emergency care, not to perform clinical management. <https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549790>
- WHO (2013), *Responding to intimate partner violence and sexual violence against women*, and its clinical safety-planning materials support assessing immediate danger, respecting survivor choice, avoiding actions that increase danger, and connecting to appropriate local services. Apply these principles inclusively without assuming gender, and do not treat an AI chat as a substitute for a trained survivor service. <https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241548595>
- WHO (2021), *Guidelines for establishing a poison centre*, describes poisoning as a time-dependent emergency requiring specialist expertise. WHO poisoning guidance advises against inducing vomiting unless directed by a poison centre or doctor. Use this only for urgent routing and safe handoff, not substance-specific treatment. <https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2021-who-guidelines-for-establishing-a-poison-centre>
- WHO, War Trauma Foundation, and World Vision International (2011), *Psychological first aid: Guide for field workers*, supports practical help with urgent needs such as safety, food, water, shelter, sanitation, healthcare, and connection to available services. <https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789241548205>
- Measurement tools developed for autistic adults may improve research or structured assessment, but an AI conversation must not convert a screening score into a safety guarantee or diagnosis. Current intent, plan, means, recent action, supports, and immediate environment require direct assessment.

## Evidence-language rules

Use calibrated language:

- `supported by a current systematic review` when directly applicable;
- `expert consensus recommends` when evidence is consensus;
- `autism research suggests` or `ADHD research suggests` when extrapolating to AuDHD;
- `a plausible hypothesis` for case formulation;
- `an experiment you could try` for low-risk accommodations without strong direct trials;
- `not enough direct evidence` when that is the honest conclusion.

Do not use publication year alone as a quality marker. Prefer design quality, population fit, clinically meaningful outcomes, autistic community involvement, and transparent limitations.
